Twelve students sat the same exam. Before it, each one wrote down how many hours they had studied. We have both numbers for all twelve, and a thirteenth student who studied for four and a half hours and wants to know what to expect.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to answer that with a number, say how far off the answer is likely to be, and explain exactly where the number came from.

## The model is a line

A straight line is fixed by two numbers. Call them $w$ and $b$:

<Equation reads="predicted score = w × hours + b">

$$
\hat{y} = w\,x + b
$$

</Equation>

Here <Symbol id="x" /> is the input (hours), and <Symbol id="yhat" /> is the prediction. The hat is there to keep it apart from <Symbol id="y" />, the score the student really got.

- $w$ is the <Term id="slope">slope</Term>: how many points one extra hour is worth.
- $b$ is the <Term id="bias">bias</Term>: the score the line gives for zero hours.

This rule, with its two adjustable numbers, is our <Term id="model">model</Term>. Different values of $w$ and $b$ give different lines, and so different predictions.

<Example title="Reading a prediction">

Suppose someone hands us the line $w = 5$, $b = 45$. For a student who studied 4 hours:

$$
\hat{y} = 5 \times 4 + 45 = 65
$$

Student B studied 4 hours and scored 66, so this line is off by one point for that student.

</Example>

## Measuring the miss

Many lines look reasonable. To pick one we need a way to score a line, so that "better" means something exact.

Start with one student. The <Term id="residual">residual</Term> is how far the line missed:

$$
\text{residual} = y - \hat{y}
$$

A positive residual means the student did better than the line said. A negative one means worse.

To score the whole line we need to combine twelve residuals into one number. Simply adding them fails, because a miss of $+10$ and a miss of $-10$ would cancel to zero and a terrible line could look perfect. So we square each residual first, then take the average. The result is the <Term id="mse">mean squared error</Term>:

<Equation reads="average, over all n students, of the squared miss">

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2
$$

</Equation>

<MarginNote>
The symbol Σ just means "add these up". The little i counts through the students, from the first to the n-th.
</MarginNote>

<Example title="Scoring a line on three students">

Take the line $w = 5$, $b = 45$ and students A, B and D from the table.

| Student | Hours | Score $y$ | Prediction $\hat{y}$ | Residual | Squared |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1 | 48 | 50 | −2 | 4 |
| B | 4 | 66 | 65 | +1 | 1 |
| D | 9 | 91 | 90 | +1 | 1 |

The mean of the squared column is $(4 + 1 + 1) / 3 = 2$. On these three students, this line has an MSE of 2.

</Example>

A number like this, which says how wrong a model is, is called a <Term id="loss">loss</Term>. Lower is better. Squaring has one more effect worth noticing: a miss of 10 costs 100, while a miss of 1 costs 1. Big misses dominate.

## The best line, exactly

In the last figure you searched for the lowest error by hand. For a straight line and squared error there is no need to search. There is a <Term id="closed-form">closed-form solution</Term>: two formulas that give the best $w$ and $b$ directly. Choosing the line this way is called <Term id="least-squares">least squares</Term>.

Write $\bar{x}$ for the average of the hours and $\bar{y}$ for the average of the scores. Then:

<Equation reads="slope = how x and y vary together, divided by how much x varies on its own">

$$
w = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}
\qquad\qquad
b = \bar{y} - w\,\bar{x}
$$

</Equation>

The second formula says something useful in plain words: the best line always passes through the point of averages, $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.

<Example title="Three points, by hand">

Take the tiny dataset $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 2)$.

1. Averages: $\bar{x} = 2$ and $\bar{y} = 5/3$.
2. Top of the fraction: $(-1)(-\tfrac{2}{3}) + (0)(\tfrac{1}{3}) + (1)(\tfrac{1}{3}) = 1$.
3. Bottom of the fraction: $(-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 = 2$.
4. Slope: $w = 1/2$. Bias: $b = 5/3 - \tfrac{1}{2}\times 2 = 2/3$.

The best line is $\hat{y} = 0.5\,x + 0.67$.

</Example>

For the twelve students the same steps give $\bar{x} = 4.625$, $\bar{y} = 67$, a top of $388$ and a bottom of $73.06$:

$$
w = \frac{388}{73.06} \approx 5.31
\qquad
b = 67 - 5.31 \times 4.625 \approx 42.44
$$

So each extra hour of study is worth about 5.3 points, and we can finally answer the opening question:

$$
\hat{y} = 5.31 \times 4.5 + 42.44 \approx 66.3
$$

<Derivation title="Where the formulas come from">

The MSE is a function of $w$ and $b$. Picture it as a surface: every choice of $(w, b)$ has a height, the error of that line. For squared error this surface is a smooth bowl, so its lowest point is where it is flat in both directions.

Flat in the $b$ direction means the derivative with respect to $b$ is zero:

$$
\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial b} = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_i (y_i - w x_i - b) = 0
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; b = \bar{y} - w\,\bar{x}
$$

Put that $b$ back into the MSE and ask for flatness in the $w$ direction:

$$
\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial w} = -\frac{2}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})\big((y_i - \bar{y}) - w (x_i - \bar{x})\big) = 0
$$

Solving that for $w$ gives the fraction above. If derivatives are new to you, the [toolkit chapter on derivatives](/derivatives-and-gradients/) covers everything used here.

</Derivation>

## How good is the fit?

The best line is still not a perfect line. Three numbers describe how well it does, each answering a slightly different question.

| Measure | Value here | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| MSE | 6.13 | The quantity we minimised. Its units are points squared, which is awkward to interpret. |
| RMSE | 2.48 | The square root of the MSE. A typical miss is about two and a half points. |
| R² | 0.966 | The line explains about 97% of the differences between students' scores. |

<Term id="rmse">Root mean squared error</Term> is the one to quote to a person, because it is in the same units as the score. Our answer to the thirteenth student is "about 66, give or take two or three points".

<Term id="r-squared">R squared</Term> compares the line with the laziest possible model: ignore the hours and predict the average score, 67, for everyone. That lazy model has an MSE of 177.8. Ours has 6.13.

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{6.13}{177.8} \approx 0.966
$$

An R² of 1 means a perfect fit and 0 means no better than the average. It can even be negative, when a model does worse than the average.

<MarginNote>
A high R² does not mean the model is right. It only means the line follows these particular dots closely.
</MarginNote>

One warning before trusting the line too far. Our students studied between 1 and 9 hours. For 12 hours the line predicts $5.31 \times 12 + 42.44 \approx 106$, on an exam marked out of 100. Predicting outside the range of the data is called <Term id="extrapolation">extrapolation</Term>, and the line has no evidence to stand on there.

## One wild point

Squaring makes big misses count much more than small ones. That has a cost. A single student far from the pattern, an <Term id="outlier">outlier</Term>, can pull the whole line toward themselves.

Imagine the student who studied 9 hours fell ill on the day and scored 35. Least squares will tilt the line a long way to reduce that one enormous squared miss, and every other prediction gets worse to pay for it.

One alternative is to average the *absolute* misses instead of the squared ones. A miss of 10 then costs 10, not 100, so no single student can dominate.

<Figure

Neither choice is always right. Squared error is smooth and has an exact formula, which is why it is the default. Absolute error is sturdier when the data contains mistakes or freak results. The first thing to do with a surprising fit is to plot the data and look for a point like this.

## In code

In practice nobody computes the two formulas by hand. Here is the whole chapter in a few lines of Python, using the scikit-learn library.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, r2_score

hours = np.array([1, 1.5, 2, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 5.5, 6, 7, 8, 9])
score = np.array([48, 53, 50, 60, 57, 66, 68, 75, 71, 82, 83, 91])

X = hours.reshape(-1, 1)             # one row per student, one column per feature
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, score)

print(model.coef_[0], model.intercept_)       # 5.31  42.44
pred = model.predict(X)
print(mean_squared_error(score, pred))        # 6.13
print(r2_score(score, pred))                  # 0.966
print(model.predict([[4.5]]))                 # [66.34]
```

The numbers match the ones we worked out, because `fit` is doing exactly the calculation from this chapter.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A linear model predicts with a straight line, $\hat{y} = w x + b$. Learning means choosing $w$ and $b$.
- A **residual** is one miss. The **mean squared error** combines all the misses into a single loss.
- For a line and squared error, the best $w$ and $b$ come from a formula. No searching is needed.
- Report **RMSE** for a typical miss in real units, and **R²** for how much better the line is than guessing the average.
- Squared error is sensitive to outliers, and no line should be trusted outside the range of its data.

</KeyTakeaways>

<GoingFurther>

### More than one feature

Real problems have several features: hours studied, hours slept, previous grade. The model gets one weight per feature, $\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_d x_d + b$, and the "line" becomes a flat sheet in more dimensions. Stack the examples into a table $X$ with one row per student (the [toolkit chapter on vectors and matrices](/vectors-and-matrices/) explains this notation), and the best weights still have a closed form, called the normal equation:

$$
w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y
$$

It is the same idea as the two formulas above, written for any number of features.

### Why squares, really?

Squaring is convenient, but there is a deeper reason. If the scores are a straight line plus random bell-shaped noise, then the least-squares line is also the most probable line given the data. The [probability toolkit chapter](/probability/) has the background.

### What the line assumes

A straight line is the right tool when the relationship really is straight, when the scatter around it is about the same everywhere, and when the examples do not influence each other. Plotting the residuals against the input is the quickest check: they should look like a shapeless cloud around zero.

</GoingFurther>
