This is a toolkit chapter, and optional. Data is noisy and predictions are uncertain, so machine learning needs a language for uncertainty. That language is probability. This chapter covers the few pieces of it the book uses.

## A number for uncertainty

A <Term id="probability">probability</Term> is a number between 0 and 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means it never happens, 1 means it always does, and 0.5 means it happens half the time. The same numbers are often written as percentages.

The most useful way to read a probability is as a long-run share. Saying a fair coin has probability 0.5 of heads is a claim about many flips: about half of them will be heads. It says very little about any single flip.

Roll two dice and add them up. The total is a <Term id="random-variable">random variable</Term>: a quantity whose value is settled by chance. Some totals are likelier than others. There is only one way to make 2, and six ways to make 7.

After ten rolls the bars look nothing like the rings. After a few thousand they match closely. That is why more data makes for better models: every estimate made from data is a share like these, and small samples are ragged.

## Average and spread

Two numbers summarise a random quantity well.

The first is where it is centred. The <Term id="expected-value">expected value</Term> is the long-run average: what you would get by averaging over a great many repeats. For two dice it is 7.

The second is how spread out it is. Take the distance of each value from the average, square it, and average those. That is the variance. Its square root, the <Term id="standard-deviation">standard deviation</Term>, is easier to read because it is back in the original units: roughly, how far a typical value sits from the average.

<Example title="Eight quiz marks">

The marks are 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9.

1. Average: $(2 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 9) / 8 = 5$.
2. Distances from 5: −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4.
3. Squared: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16. Their average is $32 / 8 = 4$. That is the variance.
4. Standard deviation: $\sqrt{4} = 2$.

A typical mark is about 2 away from the average of 5.

</Example>

<MarginNote>
Squaring should feel familiar. Mean squared error, from <Ref to="linear-regression">the linear regression chapter</Ref>, is the same calculation with "distance from the prediction" in place of "distance from the average".
</MarginNote>

## The bell curve

Many quantities in the world pile up around an average and thin out symmetrically on both sides: heights, measurement errors, exam scores. The curve that describes this is the <Term id="normal-distribution">normal distribution</Term>, the bell curve. It is fixed entirely by two numbers, its average and its standard deviation.

Whatever the average and spread, the same shares always apply:

| Within | Share of values |
|---|---|
| 1 standard deviation of the average | about 68% |
| 2 standard deviations | about 95% |
| 3 standard deviations | about 99.7% |

So if exam scores average 67 with a standard deviation of 10, about two thirds of students score between 57 and 77, and almost nobody scores below 37.

The bell curve matters to this book for a specific reason. The usual picture of noisy data is "the true pattern, plus bell-shaped noise". Under that picture, the model that makes the observed data most probable is exactly the one with the smallest squared error. That is the deeper justification for least squares.

## When something is already known

Probabilities change when you learn something. The chance that a message is spam is one number. The chance that it is spam *given that it contains the word "free"* is a different, larger number. A probability that takes known information into account is a <Term id="conditional-probability">conditional probability</Term>.

Conditioning sounds abstract and is really just narrowing your attention. Here are the 34 messages from <Ref to="naive-bayes">the Naive Bayes chapter</Ref>, sorted two ways at once.

The two answers differ because they divide the same 9 messages by different totals:

- Of the **14 spam messages**, 9 contain "free": 64%.
- Of the **10 messages containing "free"**, 9 are spam: 90%.

Confusing these two is one of the most common mistakes in reasoning with data. Converting one into the other is what Bayes' rule does, and the [Naive Bayes chapter](/naive-bayes/) builds a whole classifier on it.

Two things are <Term id="independence">independent</Term> when knowing one tells you nothing about the other, so conditioning changes nothing. Two dice are independent. "Is spam" and "contains free" clearly are not.

<Pitfall title="Reading a conditional backwards">

"Most people with the illness test positive" does not mean "most people who test positive have the illness". If the illness is rare, the positives are mostly healthy people the test got wrong. Whenever you see a "given", check which group is being counted in the bottom of the fraction. This single confusion is behind a large share of the bad statistics in news reports and courtrooms.

</Pitfall>

## In code

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                  # a seeded random generator

# Two dice, rolled 100,000 times.
totals = rng.integers(1, 7, 100_000) + rng.integers(1, 7, 100_000)
print(np.mean(totals == 7))                     # 0.167  share of sevens
print(totals.mean(), totals.std())              # 7.0  2.4  average and spread

# Exam scores from a bell curve with average 67 and spread 10.
scores = rng.normal(67, 10, 100_000)
print(np.mean(np.abs(scores - 67) < 10))        # 0.68  within one standard deviation

# Conditional probability is a share within a filtered subset.
is_spam  = np.array([1] * 14 + [0] * 20)
has_free = np.array([1] * 9 + [0] * 5 + [1] * 1 + [0] * 19)
print(has_free[is_spam == 1].mean())            # 0.64  P(free | spam)
print(is_spam[has_free == 1].mean())            # 0.90  P(spam | free)
```

<KeyTakeaways>

- A **probability** is a long-run share between 0 and 1. Small samples are ragged; large ones settle.
- The **expected value** says where a random quantity is centred; the **standard deviation** says how spread out it is.
- The **normal distribution** is fixed by those two numbers. About 68% of values fall within one standard deviation.
- A **conditional probability** narrows attention to the cases where something is known to be true.
- "Chance of A given B" and "chance of B given A" are different numbers.

</KeyTakeaways>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Eight quiz scores are 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7 and 9. Find the average, then the standard deviation (divide by the number of values).

<Hint>

Find the average first. Then average the squared distances from it, and take the square root.

</Hint>

<Solution>

The scores add up to 40, so the average is $40 / 8 = 5$.

The distances from the average are −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2 and 4. Squared, they are 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4 and 16, which add up to 32. The average squared distance is $32 / 8 = 4$, and its square root is the standard deviation: 2.

A typical score is about 2 points away from 5.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Exam scores follow a bell curve with an average of 70 and a standard deviation of 8. Roughly what share of students score between 62 and 78? Between 54 and 86? Above 86?

<Hint>

Count how many standard deviations each end of the range is from 70.

</Hint>

<Solution>

62 to 78 is one standard deviation either side of the average: about 68%.

54 to 86 is two standard deviations either side: about 95%.

That leaves about 5% outside the range 54 to 86, split evenly between the two tails, so about 2.5% score above 86.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Out of 100 emails, 30 are spam. The word "free" appears in 18 of the spam emails and in 7 of the others. What is the chance that a spam email contains "free"? What is the chance that an email containing "free" is spam?

<Hint>

The top of both fractions is the same 18 emails. Decide which group goes on the bottom each time.

</Hint>

<Solution>

Given spam, look only at the 30 spam emails: $18 / 30 = 0.6$.

Given "free", look only at the emails that contain it. There are $18 + 7 = 25$ of them, and 18 are spam: $18 / 25 = 0.72$.

The same 18 emails sit on top of both fractions. The answers differ because the bottoms differ: 30 spam emails in one case, 25 "free" emails in the other.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="in code">

Simulate rolling a die 10 times and work out the share of sixes. Run it several times. Then do the same with 10,000 rolls. What changes?

<Hint>

`rng.integers(1, 7, size=n)` gives n rolls. `(rolls == 6).mean()` gives the share of sixes.

</Hint>

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng()
for n in (10, 10_000):
    rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n)   # whole numbers from 1 to 6
    print(n, (rolls == 6).mean())
```

Your numbers will differ on every run. With 10 rolls the share of sixes jumps around, and anything from 0 to 0.4 is common. With 10,000 rolls it stays within about 0.01 of the true value, $1/6 \approx 0.167$. A probability describes the long run, and small samples are ragged.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
