This is a toolkit chapter, and optional. It covers the one idea from calculus that machine learning leans on: measuring how sensitive an output is to its inputs. Training a model means asking that question about the loss and every weight, millions of times.

## How fast is it changing?

For a straight line the question has an easy answer. The line $y = 3x + 1$ goes up 3 for every 1 it goes across. Its slope is 3, everywhere.

$$
\text{slope} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\text{change in output}}{\text{change in input}}
$$

A slope is a rate. If $x$ is hours of study and $y$ is an exam score, a slope of 3 means three points per hour. Positive slopes go uphill from left to right, negative ones downhill, and a slope of zero is flat.

Curves are harder, because a curve is steeper in some places than others. It has no single slope. It has a slope *at each point*.

## Slope by nudging

To find the slope of a curve at one point, do what you would do with a real hill: take a small step and see how much higher you are.

Pick a point $x$. Nudge it by a small amount $h$. Measure how much the output changed, and divide by the nudge:

<Equation reads="slope ≈ change in output ÷ size of the nudge">

$$
\text{slope at } x \;\approx\; \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

</Equation>

With a big nudge the line cuts across the curve and its slope is only a rough answer. As the nudge shrinks, the line stops changing: it settles onto the line that just touches the curve. The slope it settles on is the <Term id="derivative">derivative</Term> of the function at that point.

At $x = 1$ on the curve $y = x^2$, the numbers go like this:

| Nudge $h$ | 2 | 1 | 0.1 | 0.01 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rise ÷ run | 4 | 3 | 2.1 | 2.01 |

They are closing in on 2. Try other points and a pattern appears: the slope at $x$ always settles on $2x$.

## Rules instead of nudging

Nudging works for any function, and computers do sometimes find derivatives this way. But for common functions the answer follows a pattern, so it can be written down once and reused.

| Function | Its derivative | In words |
|---|---|---|
| $c$ (a constant) | $0$ | A constant never changes. |
| $c\,x$ | $c$ | A straight line has the same slope everywhere. |
| $x^2$ | $2x$ | Steeper the further you are from zero. |
| $x^n$ | $n\,x^{n-1}$ | Bring the power down, reduce it by one. |
| $f + g$ | $f' + g'$ | Do each part separately and add. |

The derivative of $f$ is written $f'(x)$, or $\dfrac{df}{dx}$. Both mean the same thing: the slope of $f$ at $x$.

<Example title="Using the rules">

Take $f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x + 7$. Handle each part in turn:

- $3x^2$ becomes $3 \times 2x = 6x$
- $5x$ becomes $5$
- $7$ becomes $0$

So $f'(x) = 6x + 5$. At $x = 2$ the curve is rising at a rate of 17.

</Example>

One fact makes derivatives useful for finding the best model. At the very bottom of a valley, or the top of a hill, the ground is flat: **the derivative is zero**. That is how the formula for the best line was found in <Ref to="linear-regression">the linear regression chapter</Ref>.

## Several inputs at once

A real loss depends on many weights, not one. With two inputs, picture the function as a landscape: every position $(x, y)$ has a height.

Standing on a hillside there are now two questions. How steep is it if I step east? And how steep if I step north? Each is answered by nudging one input while holding the other still. The result is a <Term id="partial-derivative">partial derivative</Term>, written with a curly $\partial$.

For the bowl $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 6y
$$

To get the first, treat $y$ as a constant and use the rules above. To get the second, treat $x$ as a constant.

Put the partial derivatives side by side in a vector and you have the <Term id="gradient">gradient</Term>. It has a remarkable property: it points in the direction of steepest ascent. Its opposite therefore points straight downhill.

This is the whole basis of gradient descent. Compute the gradient of the loss, step the opposite way, repeat. A model with a million weights has a gradient with a million entries, and the recipe is unchanged.

## The chain rule

Models are built by feeding one calculation into the next. A weighted sum goes into a sigmoid; the sigmoid goes into a loss. To train the model we need to know how the final output responds to something at the very start.

Consider two steps. First triple the input: $u = 3x$. Then square the result: $y = u^2$.

- Nudge $x$, and $u$ changes 3 times as much.
- Nudge $u$, and $y$ changes $2u$ times as much.

So a nudge to $x$ is magnified by 3 and then by $2u$. The overall rate is the two multiplied together. This is the <Term id="chain-rule">chain rule</Term>.

<Equation reads="overall rate = rate of the second step × rate of the first step">

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \times \frac{du}{dx}
$$

</Equation>

With three steps there are three rates to multiply, and with a hundred steps a hundred. Backpropagation, the algorithm that trains neural networks, is this rule applied carefully from the end of a long chain back to its beginning.

<Pitfall title="A nudge that is too big or too small">

Checking a derivative by nudging only works if the nudge is a sensible size. Too big, say 0.5, and you measure the slope of a long chord and not the slope at the point. Too small, say 0.000000000000001, and the computer's rounding errors swamp the tiny change in the output. A nudge around 0.00001 is a safe choice for numbers of ordinary size.

</Pitfall>

## In code

The nudging definition is three lines of Python, and it is a handy way to check a derivative you have worked out by hand.

```python
def slope(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)      # nudge both ways for accuracy

f = lambda x: 3 * x**2 + 5 * x + 7
print(slope(f, 2))                              # 17.0, matching 6x + 5 at x = 2

def gradient(f, point, h=1e-5):
    grad = []
    for i in range(len(point)):                 # nudge one input at a time
        up, down = list(point), list(point)
        up[i] += h
        down[i] -= h
        grad.append((f(up) - f(down)) / (2 * h))
    return grad

bowl = lambda p: p[0]**2 + 3 * p[1]**2
print(gradient(bowl, [2, 1]))                   # [4.0, 6.0]
```

Libraries such as PyTorch do not nudge. They apply the rules and the chain rule automatically, which is exact and far faster.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A **derivative** is the slope of a function at a point: how much the output changes per unit change in the input.
- It can be found by **nudging** the input and dividing, or by a few simple rules.
- At a lowest or highest point the derivative is **zero**.
- With several inputs there is one **partial derivative** each. Together they form the **gradient**, which points uphill.
- The **chain rule**: when calculations are chained, their rates multiply.

</KeyTakeaways>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

For $f(x) = x^2$, estimate the slope at $x = 3$ with a nudge of 0.1, then with a nudge of 0.01. What value are the estimates heading towards, and what does the rule give?

<Hint>

Work out $f(3 + \text{nudge}) - f(3)$ and divide by the nudge.

</Hint>

<Solution>

With a nudge of 0.1: $(3.1^2 - 3^2) / 0.1 = (9.61 - 9) / 0.1 = 6.1$.

With a nudge of 0.01: $(3.01^2 - 3^2) / 0.01 = (9.0601 - 9) / 0.01 = 6.01$.

The estimates are closing in on 6. The rule says the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$, which is exactly 6 at $x = 3$.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Where is the lowest point of $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 10$, and how low is it?

<Hint>

At a lowest point the derivative is zero. The derivative of $x^2 - 6x + 10$ is $2x - 6$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

At a lowest point the derivative is zero. The derivative is $2x - 6$, which is zero at $x = 3$. There, $f(3) = 9 - 18 + 10 = 1$.

So the lowest point is at $x = 3$, where the function equals 1. To the left of 3 the derivative is negative (the curve is falling) and to the right it is positive (rising), which confirms it is a lowest point and not a highest one.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Let $f(w, b) = w^2 + 3b^2$. Find the gradient at $w = 2$, $b = 1$. If you wanted to make $f$ smaller, in which direction would you move?

<Hint>

Find each partial derivative by treating the other input as a fixed number. The gradient points uphill.

</Hint>

<Solution>

Treat the other input as a fixed number each time. The partial derivative with respect to $w$ is $2w$, and with respect to $b$ it is $6b$. At the point (2, 1) the gradient is $[4,\ 6]$.

The gradient points uphill, so to make $f$ smaller move the opposite way, along $[-4,\ -6]$: decrease both $w$ and $b$, and $b$ by more, because the surface is steeper in that direction.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="by hand">

Let $y = (3x + 1)^2$. Use the chain rule to find how fast $y$ changes with $x$ at $x = 1$. Check your answer by nudging $x$ to 1.001.

<Hint>

Name the inside $u = 3x + 1$. Find how fast $u$ changes with $x$, how fast $y$ changes with $u$, and multiply.

</Hint>

<Solution>

Split the calculation into two steps: $u = 3x + 1$, then $y = u^2$. The rates are 3 for the first step and $2u$ for the second. At $x = 1$, $u = 4$, so the second rate is 8. Rates multiply along the chain: $3 \times 8 = 24$.

Check: at $x = 1.001$, $u = 4.003$ and $y = 16.024009$. The change in $y$ is 0.024009 for a nudge of 0.001, a rate of about 24.01.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise kind="with the figure">

In the figure "Which way is up?", drag the point slowly along one of the rings. What stays true about the arrow the whole way round? Then drag it to the centre. Why does the arrow vanish there?

<Hint>

A ring joins places of equal height. Think about which way you would have to walk to gain height fastest.

</Hint>

<Solution>

All the way round, the arrow crosses the ring at right angles. Along a ring the height does not change, so the direction of fastest climb can have no part lying along the ring. It must point straight across it.

At the centre the surface is at its lowest point. Every direction is uphill by the same negligible amount for a tiny step, so there is no direction of climb to point in, and the gradient is zero. This is how gradient descent knows it has arrived.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
