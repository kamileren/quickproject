So far "better" has meant "lower error on the data we have". This chapter shows why that is the wrong target, and what to aim for instead. It contains the most practically useful idea in the book: how to tell whether a model has really learned something or has only memorised its examples.

## A model that memorises

Imagine a model that is nothing but a lookup table. It stores every training example. Asked about a student it has seen, it returns that student's exact score. Its training error is zero. No model can beat it.

Now ask it about a student it has not seen. It has nothing to say. A perfect score on the training data told us nothing about whether it could do the job.

This extreme case is a warning about every model. Training error measures how well a model matches examples it was *shown*. What we care about is how it does on examples it was *not* shown. The gap between the two is what this chapter is about.

## Too simple, too flexible

A new dataset makes the problem visible. Twelve students recorded how long they slept the night before an exam, and their score. Too little sleep hurts. So, it turns out, does too much.

A straight line cannot follow a pattern that rises and then falls. So we let the model bend: instead of a line, fit a curve. The *degree* of the curve sets how many bends it is allowed. Degree 1 is a straight line, degree 2 has one bend, and degree 9 can wiggle through almost anything.

Three things happen as you raise the degree.

At **degree 1** the line misses the shape entirely. This is <Term id="underfitting">underfitting</Term>: the model is too simple for the pattern, and it is wrong about fitted and new students alike.

At **degree 2** the curve follows the rise and fall. It does not pass through every dot, and it should not, because the dots include luck. That unpredictable part is called <Term id="noise">noise</Term>.

At **degree 9** the curve bends to pass close to every fitted student. Its error on them is tiny. But it gets there by swinging wildly between the dots, and the new students land nowhere near it. This is <Term id="overfitting">overfitting</Term>: the model has learned the noise in these twelve students as if it were the pattern.

## Training error always falls

The error on the fitted students only ever goes down: from 66 at degree 1, to 12 at degree 2, to 5 at degree 9. A more flexible model can always match its own data at least as well.

The error on new students tells the truth. It drops from 102 to 29 as the model becomes able to follow the pattern, stays roughly level for a while, then climbs steeply to 192 at degree 9.

The best model is at the bottom of the orange curve, not the dark one. Choosing by training error would pick degree 9, the worst model on the page.

<MarginNote>
The noise in this data has a spread of about 5 points, so no model could have an error on new students much below 25. Degree 2 reaches 29. It is nearly as good as any model can be.
</MarginNote>

## Three piles of data

To choose a degree we looked at the new students. That is a problem. Once we have used them to make a decision, they are no longer "new", and their error is no longer an honest estimate of real-world performance.

The fix is to split the data into three piles before doing anything else.

| Pile | Used for | Typical share |
|---|---|---|
| Training set | Fitting the weights | 60 to 80% |
| Validation set | Choosing between models and settings | 10 to 20% |
| Test set | One final, honest measurement | 10 to 20% |

The <Term id="validation-set">validation set</Term> is the one you are allowed to look at repeatedly. The test set is opened once, at the very end.

A setting like the degree, which you choose rather than learn, is called a <Term id="hyperparameter">hyperparameter</Term>. The learning rate from <Ref to="gradient-descent">the last chapter</Ref> is another. Hyperparameters are tuned on the validation set.

One rule protects all of this. Nothing about the validation or test examples may influence training: not their labels, and not even their averages if you rescale features. When it does, the result is called <Term id="leakage">data leakage</Term>, and the model will look better on paper than it is.

## Cross-validation

Three piles are expensive when data is scarce. With twelve students, setting four aside leaves only eight to learn from, and a validation set of two or three students is mostly luck.

<Term id="cross-validation">Cross-validation</Term> gets more out of the same data. Split the students into five groups. Hide the first group, fit on the other four, and score the fit on the hidden group. Then hide the second group instead, and so on. Every student is used for checking exactly once and for fitting four times. The average of the five scores is the model's cross-validation score.

## Bias and variance

There is a tidy way to describe the two ways of going wrong.

Suppose we could repeat the whole study with a different class of twelve students, again and again, fitting the same kind of model each time.

- A rigid model would give nearly the same answer every time, and every time it would miss the real shape. That consistent miss is called <Term id="model-bias">bias</Term>.
- A very flexible model would give a wildly different curve for each class, because it chases each class's noise. That instability is called <Term id="variance">variance</Term>.

At degree 1 the lines barely move, and all of them are wrong in the same way: high bias, low variance. At degree 8 the curves fly all over the plot: low bias, very high variance. Degree 2 or 3 gets both small at once.

Making a model more flexible lowers bias and raises variance. The skill is in finding the point between them, and more data helps too: the more students each class has, the less any flexible model can be fooled by their noise.

<Pitfall title="Just one look at the test set">

Nobody plans to tune on the test set. It happens one look at a time. You check the test score, change a setting, and check again, and each look seems harmless. After twenty looks you have picked the settings that happen to suit those particular test examples, and the score is no longer an honest estimate. Make every decision with the validation set or cross-validation. Open the test set once, at the very end.

</Pitfall>

## In code

scikit-learn does the splitting and the cross-validation. Here the degree is chosen by cross-validation on the training data, and the test set is used once at the end.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler

def model(degree):
    # The pipeline learns its scaling from the training folds only: no leakage.
    return make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree), StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())

scores = {}
for degree in range(1, 10):
    cv = cross_val_score(model(degree), X_train, y_train, cv=5,
                         scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
    scores[degree] = -cv.mean()                 # average error over the five rounds

best = min(scores, key=scores.get)              # lowest cross-validation error
final = model(best).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(best, np.mean((final.predict(X_test) - y_test) ** 2))   # the one honest number
```

<KeyTakeaways>

- Training error always improves with a more flexible model. It cannot tell you when to stop.
- **Underfitting** is being too simple for the pattern. **Overfitting** is fitting the noise.
- Judge models on data they have not seen: tune on a **validation set**, report on a **test set** used once.
- **Cross-validation** rotates the hold-out so that small datasets are used fully.
- Flexibility trades **bias** for **variance**. The best model keeps both small.

</KeyTakeaways>

You have 20 examples and run 5-fold cross-validation. In each round, how many examples are trained on and how many are held out? How many models are trained in total, and how many times is each example held out? What would the answers be if every fold held just one example?

<Solution>

Five folds of 4 examples each. Every round trains on 16 and holds out 4. Five models are trained, and each example is held out exactly once.

With one example per fold there are 20 folds. Every round trains on 19 and holds out 1, and 20 models are trained. This is called leave-one-out cross-validation. It uses the data as fully as possible and costs the most computing.

<Exercise kind="think">

A friend trains 50 different models, measures each on the test set, and reports the best score, 91% accurate. What is wrong with that number, and what should they have done?

<Solution>

The test set was used to choose among 50 models, so it is no longer unseen data. Some of those models scored well partly by luck on those particular examples, and picking the best one picks the luckiest. On fresh data the chosen model will probably score below 91%.

They should have compared the 50 models on a validation set, or by cross-validation, and then measured only the winner on the test set, once.

<Exercise kind="in code">

Make 15 noisy training points from a sine curve, and 200 new points the same way. Fit curves of degree 1, 3 and 9 to the training points, and print the error of each on both sets.

<Solution>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def sample(n):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 6, n)
    return x, np.sin(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)

x_train, y_train = sample(15)
x_new, y_new = sample(200)

for degree in (1, 3, 9):
    curve = np.polyfit(x_train, y_train, degree)
    train = np.mean((np.polyval(curve, x_train) - y_train) ** 2)
    new = np.mean((np.polyval(curve, x_new) - y_new) ** 2)
    print(degree, round(train, 3), round(new, 3))
```

With this seed the output is:

```
1 0.154 0.422
3 0.04 0.101
9 0.023 1.853
```

Training error falls every time the degree goes up. Error on new points falls from degree 1 to 3 and then explodes at degree 9. The noise has a variance of $0.3^2 = 0.09$, so no model can do much better than 0.09 on new points, and degree 3 is already close.

</Solution>

<GoingFurther>

### Splitting data that has structure

A random split assumes examples are independent. If the data is ordered in time, split by time, training on the past and testing on the future. If several rows belong to the same person, keep each person entirely in one pile. Otherwise the model is tested on something it has effectively already seen.

### When more flexibility helps again

Very large neural networks behave in a way this chapter does not predict: past the point where they can memorise the training data, error on new data often starts falling again. This is called double descent. The classical picture here is still the right guide for the models in the first three parts of this book.
