The data is the exam class again, all eighteen students this time, and for each one a single fact: did they pass?

## A line is the wrong shape

The obvious first move is to reuse linear regression. Write a pass as 1 and a fail as 0, and fit a straight line through those numbers. The height of the line can then be read as the chance of passing.

The line gives a student who did no studying a −13% chance of passing, and one who studied nine hours a 124% chance. Neither number means anything. A <Term id="probability">probability</Term> has to stay between 0 and 1, and a straight line never stops.

What the data calls for is a curve that stays near 0 for a while, rises through the middle, and levels off near 1. The second option in the figure has that shape.

## Squashing a line into a probability

We do not need to give up the line. We keep it, and pass its output through a function that squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. That function is the <Term id="sigmoid">sigmoid</Term>, written $\sigma$:

<Equation reads="chance of passing = sigmoid of (w × hours + b)">

$$
p = \sigma(w\,x + b) \qquad\text{where}\qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

</Equation>

The inside, $w x + b$, is the same line as in the [linear regression chapter](/linear-regression/). Call its value the score. The sigmoid turns the score into a probability:

| Score $w x + b$ | −4 | −2 | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Probability | 2% | 12% | 50% | 88% | 98% |

A score of zero means "could go either way". Large positive scores mean "almost certainly yes", and large negative ones "almost certainly no". This model is called <Term id="logistic-regression">logistic regression</Term>. Despite the name, it is used for classification.

The curve crosses 50% exactly where the score is zero, which is at x = −b / w.
</MarginNote>

## Scoring a probability

To fit the curve we need a loss, and squared error is a poor fit for probabilities. Think about what a good prediction is here. If a student passed, the model should have given a high chance of passing. If they failed, a low one. So score the model by **the probability it gave to what actually happened**, and punish it more the smaller that was.

The standard choice is the <Term id="log-loss">log loss</Term>: take that probability and compute its negative logarithm.

| Model gave what happened a chance of | 90% | 50% | 10% | 1% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loss | 0.11 | 0.69 | 2.30 | 4.61 |

A confident, correct prediction costs almost nothing. A coin-flip costs 0.69. A confident, *wrong* prediction is punished severely, and the cost keeps growing without limit as the model becomes more sure of the wrong answer.

<Figure

  caption="The curve is the loss for one student, for every probability the model might have predicted."

>

</Figure>

For a whole dataset the loss is the average over all the students:

$$
L = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\,y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\Big]
$$

The formula looks heavy, but it is only the table above written compactly. When $y_i = 1$ the second term vanishes and the loss is $-\log p_i$. When $y_i = 0$ the first vanishes and the loss is $-\log(1 - p_i)$.

## Fitting the curve

Unlike the straight line, this model has no formula for the best $w$ and $b$. That is no obstacle, because chapter 3 gave us a method that needs only a loss and its slope. Start with $w = 0$ and $b = 0$, which predicts 50% for everyone and has a loss of 0.69, and walk downhill.

The slopes turn out to be almost the same as for linear regression:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i)\,x_i
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i)
$$

Again it is "prediction minus truth, times the input, averaged". The only difference is that the prediction $p_i$ now comes out of the sigmoid.

The run settles on $w = 1.56$ and $b = -6.03$, with a loss of 0.259. That answers the opening question:

$$
p = \sigma(1.56 \times 4.5 - 6.03) = \sigma(0.99) \approx 0.73
$$

A student who studied four and a half hours has about a 73% chance of passing. The straight-line model could only say "pass". This one says how sure it is.

## From probability to decision

Often a probability is not enough and a decision is needed: admit or not, flag or not. The usual rule is to answer yes when the probability is at least 50%. That cut-off is called the <Term id="threshold">threshold</Term>.

For our model, the probability reaches 50% at 3.85 hours. Students above that are predicted to pass, and the rule is right about 16 of the 18. The point where the answer flips is the <Term id="decision-boundary">decision boundary</Term>.

With two features the same idea gives a boundary that is a line. Here are forty students with both their hours of study and their hours of sleep.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the boundary is always straight, however the threshold moves. Logistic regression is a <Term id="linear-classifier">linear classifier</Term>: it can only divide the plane with a straight cut. Second, moving the threshold never makes the model better or worse at telling the classes apart. It only changes which mistake it prefers to make. Choosing well between those mistakes is the subject of the next chapter.

## In code

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

hours  = np.array([0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5, 6, 6.5, 7, 8, 8.5, 9])
passed = np.array([0,   0, 0,   0, 0,   0, 1, 0,   1, 0,   1, 1,   1, 1,   1, 1, 1,   1])
X = hours.reshape(-1, 1)

# penalty=None fits plain logistic regression, with no regularization.
model = LogisticRegression(penalty=None).fit(X, passed)
print(model.coef_[0][0], model.intercept_[0])      # 1.56  -6.03

print(model.predict_proba([[4.5]])[0, 1])          # 0.73: the chance of a pass
print(model.predict([[4.5]]))                      # [1]: the decision at a 50% threshold
print(model.score(X, passed))                      # 0.889: 16 of 18 right
```

`predict_proba` gives the probability; `predict` applies the 50% threshold for you. By default scikit-learn adds a ridge penalty to logistic regression, which is why it is switched off here to match the chapter.

<KeyTakeaways>

- For a yes/no question, predict a **probability**. A straight line cannot do that; a line passed through the **sigmoid** can.
- **Log loss** scores the probability given to what actually happened, and punishes confident mistakes hardest.
- There is no formula for the best weights, so the model is fitted by **gradient descent**. The gradient has the same form as for linear regression.
- A **threshold** turns the probability into a decision. 50% is a default, not a law.
- The decision boundary of logistic regression is always **straight**.

</KeyTakeaways>

<GoingFurther>

### When the classes separate perfectly

If one cut-off divides the passes from the fails with no mistakes, the data is <Term id="separable">separable</Term>, and something odd happens. The model can always lower the loss a little more by making the curve steeper, so the weights grow without limit and training never settles. A small ridge penalty from the last chapter fixes it, which is one reason libraries apply one by default.

### More than two classes

For three or more categories the sigmoid is replaced by a function called softmax, which turns one score per class into probabilities that add up to 1. The loss is the same idea: minus the log of the probability given to the correct class. Softmax appears in the neural network part of the book.

### What the weight means

In linear regression a weight is "points per hour". Here the score $w x + b$ is the logarithm of the odds of passing, so each extra hour multiplies the odds by $e^{w}$. With $w = 1.56$ that is a factor of about 4.8 per hour.
