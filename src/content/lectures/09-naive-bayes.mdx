Logistic regression learns a boundary. Nearest neighbours compares with stored examples. This chapter's classifier works a third way: it reasons about evidence. It asks how well each possible answer would explain what it sees, and it does so with nothing more than counting.

## Reasoning backwards

Start with a question people reliably get wrong. A test for an illness catches 90% of the people who have it, and wrongly flags 9% of the people who do not. You test positive. How likely is it that you are ill?

Most people say about 90%. The right answer depends on something the question left out: how common the illness is.

When 10% of people are ill, 90 ill people test positive and so do 81 healthy ones. A positive result means a 53% chance of illness, not 90%. When only 1% are ill, the healthy false alarms swamp the real cases and a positive result means about 9%.

The 90% was the answer to a different question: *if I am ill, how likely is a positive test?* We wanted the reverse: *given a positive test, how likely am I ill?* Turning one into the other is the job of <Term id="bayes-rule">Bayes' rule</Term>, and the figure just did it by counting. It needs two ingredients:

- The <Term id="prior">prior</Term>: how likely the answer was before any evidence. Here, how common the illness is.
- How well the evidence fits each answer: how often ill people, and healthy people, test positive.

The result, the updated belief, is called the <Term id="posterior">posterior</Term>.

## One word as evidence

Now swap the illness for spam and the test for a word. Our data is a small inbox of 34 messages, 14 of them spam. So before reading a message, the chance that it is spam is $14/34$, about 41%. That is the prior.

Take the word "free". Count the messages that contain it:

| | Contains "free" | Out of | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spam | 9 | 14 | 64% |
| Normal | 1 | 20 | 5% |

Each share is a <Term id="likelihood">likelihood</Term>: how probable the evidence is under one answer. (The [probability toolkit chapter](/probability/) has more on probabilities that depend on what is known.) "Free" is about thirteen times more common in spam, so seeing it should shift our belief strongly.

<Example title="Bayes' rule with the counts">

Imagine the 34 messages. Of the 14 spam, 9 contain "free". Of the 20 normal ones, 1 does. So 10 messages contain "free", and 9 of them are spam:

$$
P(\text{spam} \mid \text{free}) = \frac{9}{9 + 1} = 90\%
$$

One word moved the estimate from 41% to 90%.

</Example>

As a formula, Bayes' rule says the same thing:

<Equation reads="chance of spam given the word = prior × how well spam explains the word, divided by the total chance of seeing the word">

$$
P(\text{spam} \mid \text{word}) = \frac{P(\text{spam}) \times P(\text{word} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{word})}
$$

</Equation>

## Many words, one naive assumption

A message has many words, and we want to use all of them. Doing that exactly would mean knowing how likely every *combination* of words is in spam, and there are far too many combinations to count.

So we make a bold simplification: pretend that each word appears independently of the others, given the kind of message. Then the evidence from separate words can simply be multiplied together.

$$
P(\text{spam} \mid \text{message}) \;\propto\; P(\text{spam}) \times P(w_1 \mid \text{spam}) \times P(w_2 \mid \text{spam}) \times \dots
$$

This is the <Term id="naive-bayes">Naive Bayes</Term> classifier. The assumption of <Term id="independence">independence</Term> is plainly false. "Prize" is far more likely when "win" is already there. That is why the method is called naive. It turns out to work well anyway, for reasons we will come to.

Multiplying many small numbers is awkward, so in practice we add their logarithms instead. Each word then contributes a *push*: positive toward spam, negative toward normal. The pushes add up to a score, and the score becomes a probability exactly as in logistic regression.

The message "free lunch tomorrow" contains the spammiest common word in the inbox, yet scores as normal. "Free" pushes up by 1.99, but "lunch" and "tomorrow" push down by 1.18 and 1.59, and the starting point is already slightly negative because most messages are not spam. The model weighs all the evidence, not just the alarming part.

<MarginNote>
Words such as "today" barely move the score. They are about equally common in both kinds of message, so they carry almost no evidence.
</MarginNote>

## The word never seen

There is a trap in multiplying. The word "meeting" appears in four normal messages and in no spam at all. Its count in spam is zero, so its likelihood under spam is zero, and anything multiplied by zero is zero. A single such word makes a message *impossible* to be spam, whatever else it says.

That is far too strong a conclusion from 14 spam messages. Not having seen something is weak evidence that it never happens.

The standard fix is <Term id="smoothing">smoothing</Term>: add 1 to every count before computing the shares. A word never seen in spam is then treated as rare in spam, not impossible.

$$
P(\text{word} \mid \text{spam}) = \frac{\text{count of the word in spam} + 1}{\text{total words in spam} + \text{number of different words}}
$$

For "meeting" this gives $(0 + 1) / (79 + 68) \approx 0.7\%$ instead of zero.

With smoothing the message is judged about 95% likely to be spam. Without it, "meeting" has an infinite push the other way and the answer is 0%.

## Why something so naive works

The independence assumption is wrong, so the probabilities Naive Bayes produces are wrong too. Related words get counted as separate pieces of evidence when they are really one, which makes the model overconfident: it says 99.9% where 90% would be honest.

But classification needs only the right *side* of 50%, not the right number. Overcounting evidence usually pushes the score further in the direction it was already going. The ranking of messages stays sensible even when the probabilities are exaggerated.

The simplicity also buys real advantages:

- **Training is one pass of counting.** No gradient descent, no learning rate, nothing to tune except the smoothing.
- **It needs little data.** Each word's counts are estimated separately, so a few dozen examples already give a usable model.
- **It handles thousands of features** without trouble, where nearest neighbours would be lost.

Its weakness is the one already named: when features are strongly related, or when you need trustworthy probabilities, logistic regression is usually the better choice.

<Pitfall title="Multiplying hundreds of small numbers">

A long message means multiplying hundreds of probabilities, each well below 1. A hundred words at 1% each gives a product of $10^{-200}$. Two hundred words give $10^{-400}$, which is smaller than the smallest number a computer can store, so it is rounded to exactly zero. Both classes then score zero and the comparison is meaningless. Always add the logarithms instead. The sum for a hundred such words is about −460, an ordinary number, and the larger sum still wins.

</Pitfall>

## In code

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# texts: a list of message strings.   is_spam: 1 for spam, 0 for normal.
model = make_pipeline(
    CountVectorizer(),            # turns each message into word counts
    MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0),     # alpha is the smoothing: add 1 to every count
)
model.fit(texts, is_spam)

print(model.predict(["free lunch tomorrow"]))          # [0]: normal
print(model.predict_proba(["free prize"])[0, 1])       # 0.97: chance of spam
```

`CountVectorizer` builds the table of word counts and `MultinomialNB` applies Bayes' rule to it. Training on a million messages takes seconds.

<KeyTakeaways>

- **Bayes' rule** turns "how likely is this evidence if the answer is yes" into "how likely is yes given this evidence". It needs the **prior**.
- Rare things stay unlikely even after strong evidence. Ignoring how rare they are is the classic mistake.
- **Naive Bayes** treats every feature as independent evidence and multiplies, or adds logs.
- **Smoothing** stops one unseen word from vetoing everything else.
- It is fast, needs little data and ranks well, but its probabilities are **overconfident**.

</KeyTakeaways>

An inbox has 10 spam messages and 20 normal ones. The spam messages contain 100 words in total, with "prize" appearing 6 times and "meeting" once. The normal messages contain 200 words, with "prize" appearing once and "meeting" 10 times. Classify the message "prize meeting" without smoothing.

<Hint>

For each class, multiply the prior by the share of each word. Then compare the two products.

</Hint>

<Solution>

Priors: $10/30 = 1/3$ for spam and $2/3$ for normal.

Word shares in spam: "prize" $6/100 = 0.06$, "meeting" $1/100 = 0.01$. In normal: "prize" $1/200 = 0.005$, "meeting" $10/200 = 0.05$.

Multiply the prior by the share of each word:

- Spam: $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 0.06 \times 0.01 = 0.0002$
- Normal: $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 0.005 \times 0.05 \approx 0.000167$

Spam has the larger score. As a probability, $0.0002 / (0.0002 + 0.000167) \approx 55\%$. The two words pull in opposite directions and nearly cancel, so the model is barely more sure than a coin.

</Solution>

In the same inbox the word "lottery" appears 3 times in spam and never in normal messages. The inbox uses 50 different words. What share does "lottery" get in each class without smoothing, and with it? Why does the difference matter?

<Hint>

Smoothing adds 1 to the count on top and the number of different words to the total underneath.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**Without smoothing.** Spam: $3 / 100 = 0.03$. Normal: $0 / 200 = 0$.

**With smoothing.** Spam: $(3 + 1) / (100 + 50) \approx 0.027$. Normal: $(0 + 1) / (200 + 50) = 0.004$.

Without smoothing, the zero wipes out the whole product for the normal class. Any message containing "lottery" is declared spam with complete certainty, whatever else it says. With smoothing, "lottery" is still strong evidence for spam, about seven times more likely there, but other words can outweigh it.

</Solution>

<GoingFurther>

### Numbers instead of words

For numeric features, such as height or temperature, there is nothing to count. Gaussian Naive Bayes instead fits a bell curve to each feature within each class, and uses the height of the curve as the likelihood. Everything else is unchanged.

### Models that describe the data

Naive Bayes is a *generative* model: it learns what each class looks like, and classifies by asking which class best explains the input. Logistic regression is *discriminative*: it learns only the boundary between classes. Generative models can cope with missing features and tiny datasets; discriminative ones usually win when data is plentiful.

</GoingFurther>
