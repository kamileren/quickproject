## A list of numbers

A student studied for 4 hours and slept for 7. Write those two facts side by side:

$$
x = [4,\ 7]
$$

That list is a <Term id="vector">vector</Term>. The order matters: the first entry is always study, the second always sleep. A single ordinary number, by contrast, is called a <Term id="scalar">scalar</Term>.

Vectors are how machine learning describes things. A student is two numbers. A house might be five: size, age, rooms, floor, distance to the centre. A small greyscale image of 28 by 28 pixels is a list of 784 brightness values. Whatever the object, once it is a vector the same mathematics applies.

A vector with two entries can also be drawn, as an arrow from the origin to the point $(4, 7)$. Pictures like that are a good guide to intuition even though real vectors have far too many entries to draw.

## Adding and stretching

Two things can be done to vectors with almost no thought.

**Add** two vectors by adding matching entries. **Stretch** a vector by multiplying every entry by the same scalar.

$$
[3,\ 1] + [1,\ 3] = [4,\ 4] \qquad\qquad 2 \times [3,\ 1] = [6,\ 2]
$$

In the picture, adding means placing one arrow at the tip of the other. Stretching changes an arrow's length, and a negative scalar flips it round.

## The dot product

The most important operation in this book takes two vectors and returns a single number. Multiply matching entries, then add up the results. That is the <Term id="dot-product">dot product</Term>.

<Equation reads="multiply each pair of matching entries, then add everything up">

$$
a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_d b_d
$$

</Equation>

<Example title="A prediction is a dot product">

Suppose each hour of study is worth 5 points and each hour of sleep is worth 2. Collect those into a vector of weights, $w = [5,\ 2]$. For the student with $x = [4,\ 7]$:

$$
w \cdot x = 5 \times 4 + 2 \times 7 = 20 + 14 = 34
$$

Every linear model in this book, and every neuron in a neural network, makes its prediction this way: a dot product of weights and features, plus a bias.

</Example>

The dot product also has a geometric meaning. It measures how much two arrows point the same way.

- **Positive**: the arrows point broadly the same way.
- **Zero**: they are at right angles. Neither says anything about the other.
- **Negative**: they point broadly opposite ways.

## A table of numbers

A dataset has many examples. Stack their vectors as rows and you get a table: one row per student, one column per feature. A table of numbers is a <Term id="matrix">matrix</Term>.

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 4 & 7 \\ 6 & 5 \\ 9 & 8 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad \text{four students, two features each}
$$

Multiplying a matrix by a vector means taking the dot product of *each row* with the vector. The result has one number per row. With the students as rows and the weights as the vector, one multiplication produces every student's prediction at once.

This is why linear algebra is everywhere in machine learning. A model's predictions for a million examples are written $Xw$, three symbols, and a computer can carry that out far faster than a loop over the rows.

## Keeping track of shapes

The size of a matrix is its <Term id="shape">shape</Term>, written rows × columns. The matrix above is 4 × 2.

For $Xw$ to make sense, each row of $X$ must have as many entries as $w$, because they are paired off in a dot product. The rule is:

$$
(\,n \times d\,)\ \text{matrix} \;\times\; (\,d\,)\ \text{vector} \;=\; (\,n\,)\ \text{vector}
$$

The inner sizes must match, and they disappear. The outer size is what remains. When code involving matrices fails, mismatched shapes are the cause more often than anything else, so it is worth writing the shapes down before blaming the mathematics.

## In code

NumPy is the standard Python library for vectors and matrices. The `@` symbol means "matrix multiply", which for two vectors is the dot product.

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([4, 7])                # one student: hours studied, hours slept
w = np.array([5, 2])                # one weight per feature

print(x + x)                        # [ 8 14]   add entry by entry
print(2 * x)                        # [ 8 14]   stretch
print(w @ x)                        # 34        dot product

X = np.array([[1, 6],
              [4, 7],
              [6, 5],
              [9, 8]])              # four students as rows
print(X.shape)                      # (4, 2)
print(X @ w)                        # [17 34 40 61]   every prediction at once
```

<KeyTakeaways>

- A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers. It describes one example by its features.
- Vectors are added entry by entry, and stretched by multiplying every entry by a scalar.
- The **dot product** multiplies matching entries and adds them up. A linear model's prediction is a dot product.
- A **matrix** is a table with one row per example. Matrix times vector is one dot product per row.
- **Shapes** must match on the inside: (n × d) times (d) gives (n).

</KeyTakeaways>
